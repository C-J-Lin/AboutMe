# My Story, From High School Founder Till Now

Adversity has a way of sharpening you, but more than that, it leaves you with a permanent unease, a hunger that won't quit. I learned early that no one was coming to save me, so I'd better save myself. Work ethic became my method. Building became my therapy. I lived in 6 different homes as a kid between China, Taiwan, and the United States - I didn't really have stability, so I craved the next best thing, momentum. 

In high school, I co-founded AutoCompanion, a B2B financial software platform for automotive deals, born from pure necessity. The clock speed was insane: waking up at 6 AM for school, then juggling 100+ outbound calls and thousands of texts, and emails per day. We weren't just building software; we were selling cars with it. I jumped between roles: negotiating with JD Power, architecting the technical formulas that powered our platform, building our CRM from scratch, designing the website, writing SOPs, recruiting the team, and training them to quote deals. We scaled from $5,000 to $100,000 in monthly sales within a year, from two people to five. I sold thousands of cars during this time. In hindsight, this was the most fun I ever had in my life: the rush of building something from nothing, of watching it work. I still have life-long friendships with clients and suppliers, friends I've couch-surfed with or gone on vacation with.

Then NYU's Abu Dhabi program accepted me, a full-ride merit scholarship to join the 3% who make it in, producing more Oxford Rhodes Scholars per student than any other university on the planet. That was a bizarre twist of fate and a blessing. Supply chain shocks hit the automotive industry hard during COVID. Our supply and revenue dropped over 20%. The founding team faced difficult choices. Our CEO brought on a childhood friend as full-time staff, and suddenly, the calculus around my compensation and remote work from overseas didn't add up. I was let go at what ended up being a generous valuation. It stung, badly. But I took it as my ticket to college and the world's education I was about to receive.

Here's the thing about burning out fast and early: the relationships you build matter more than the outcome. Years later, my former CEO texted me an apology, saying things got out of hand too quickly. That text mattered. Not because I needed vindication, but because it proved what I'd always believed: when you show up fully, even messy endings don't erase the impact you made.

But that startup left me one final gift. During my time there, I had sold a car to a VC general partner and his associate. They saw something: the hustle, the sales instinct, the entrepreneurial fire, and thought it might translate to venture capital. So when I landed in Abu Dhabi, halfway across the world from Pennsylvania, I joined 7BC Venture Capital. The timing was perfect. The fund was pursuing a new Saudi Arabian-based fund with a local partner, planning to launch a fund and startup foundry concept, and needed eyes on the ground. Over two years, I evaluated 50+ investments in deep-tech, FinTech, SaaS, aerospace, and space. I led sector research on deals like Umbra, a company building a global satellite constellation, and contributed to a placement memorandum for a $125M fund to be matched by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund under Vision 2030.

But here's what I really learned: The kid who grew up bouncing between Asia, America, and the Middle East developed pattern recognition for people who build under pressure. At 7BC, I saw it in founders who reminded me of my 17-year-old self: the ones who didn't wait for perfect conditions, who built the thing they needed because no one else would. The rush of being able to interact with founders, hear their stories and motivations, was what I needed to confirm that VC was the space I wanted to be in. I learned to distinguish between founders who talk smoothly versus founders who follow through when supply chains collapse or co-founders exit. That instinct for who thrives in chaos versus who just survives it, that's what I'm still chasing.

Yet my passion for automotive never died. When school took me to NYU Stern, I returned to it at BMW's Rolls-Royce Financial Services in New Jersey, building Python and SQL pipelines to analyze VIN-level data and model subsidization strategies. Then came Golub Capital, one of the largest private credit and private equity funds in the world at $70B+ AUM, where I automated portfolio analysis workflows and built fund reporting infrastructure. These weren't just technical exercises. I wanted to understand how institutional capital thinks, how billion-dollar funds make decisions at scale.

And here's where the non-linear journey gets interesting: automotive never let me go, and I never let it go either. Throughout school and after, I kept working in the space, flipping cars for profit using insider knowledge to find deals, consulting on projects like new firms and dealers, building relationships in the industry, the world over. People started offering me roles. The industry I'd entered as a 17-year-old hustler selling cars and the software to sell them now saw me as someone who understood both the product and the finance, both the sales floor and the cap table. I chose to stay independent, to keep building rather than join someone else's playbook. I flip cars, find arbitrage opportunities in commodities like gold, using FinTech to earn profit from actual bullion purchases, whatever keeps me moving forward.

During my time at NYU, I studied finance and economics, plus coursework in political science and computer science. I had the pleasure of working with the founding team at Valicit on select projects for their GTM and software, blockchain ticketing for events focused on capturing secondary marketplace revenue. That team is now behind the Y-Combinator startup Metorial, Vercel for MCP. At school, I also wrote two capstone projects: a white paper where I interviewed 20+ C-Suite executives about aviation, and a second working paper utilizing AI/ML for creating financial models. I've been lucky enough to serve as a teaching assistant for MBA students in a class about FDI, geopolitics, and corporate strategy in the Middle East with NYU Stern's Vice Dean J. Timmons.

Today, I'm at an inflection point. I've proven I can build (AutoCompanion), evaluate (7BC), and operate at institutional scale (Golub Capital). I still get pulled back into automotive, people offering jobs, founders asking for intros, and even dealerships wanting advice. But the hunger that's driven me since childhood isn't satisfied by doing one more deal or taking one more role. I want to be the person who spots the next founder like my 17-year-old self, who backs them when it's not obvious yet, who shows up when the supply chains collapse and the co-founders exit.

And I'm still building. I'm currently working on a project in the defense and geospatial intelligence space, focused on automating satellite image analysis. Here's the use case: imagine you're a hedge fund analyst tracking Walmart parking lots to predict quarterly earnings, or a defense analyst monitoring equipment movements at known rendezvous points. Satellites make different passes over these locations, meaning each image has a different orientation. My project automates the spatial alignment and change detection that analysts currently do manually, orienting images, tagging reference points, and tracking changes over time. It's solving a real problem that used to require high-resolution prints spread across tables or endless screen toggling.

Overall, I've had a non-linear journey, but my methodology has stayed the same: find a problem, build the solution, make it work. I learned early that the world won't give you stability; you build it yourself, one deal at a time, one line of code at a time. That permanent unease from my childhood, it never left. It became the engine.

I'm drawn to Hummingbird because you back founders with that same hunger, the ones with unreasonable ambitions who don't fit the mold, who are on a mission to prove others wrong. You partner early, you don't meddle, and you show up during the near-death moments when others walk away. That's the kind of investing I want to do: identifying the 0.1% before it's obvious, supporting them through chaos, and being the call they make at 2 AM when things are falling apart. I've been that founder. Now I want to back them.
